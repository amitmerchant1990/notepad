We live in a pretty strange time for note-taking. A few years ago, a note-taking app was mostly a place where you wrote things down. Today, many of them can transcribe meetings, summarize conversations, organize documents, find connections between ideas and let you ask questions about everything you've ever written.

And I think all of that is fascinating. I use AI regularly, and I'm not particularly interested in pretending that technology hasn't changed how we work.

But when I actually need to write something down, I still often want the simplest thing possible: a plain notepad.

## The best note-taking app can sometimes feel like a blank page

There's a very practical reason for this. When something occurs to me, I don't necessarily want to think about where it belongs. I want to capture it before I forget it.

Maybe it's an idea for an article. Maybe it's a command I need while I'm working on something. Maybe it's a list of things I need to pick up later. Sometimes it's just a sentence that I don't want to lose.

A plain text notepad is almost frictionless for this. There is no decision about templates, tags, folders or databases. The page is already there, waiting for you.

## AI is good at what happens after the writing

One thing I find interesting about AI is that it changes the value of unstructured information. You don't necessarily have to organize everything perfectly at the moment you write it because an AI tool can often help you make sense of it later.

A messy collection of thoughts can become an outline. A long conversation can become a summary. A rough paragraph can become a polished one. A pile of notes can be searched and turned into something useful.

That makes me less interested in trying to build the perfect structure while I'm capturing a thought.

I'd rather write naturally first and worry about organization when there is an actual reason to organize something.

> The capture step doesn't have to be intelligent. It just has to be easy.

## I don't want every thought processed

There's also a more personal reason I prefer simple note-taking. Not everything I write is supposed to become something.

Sometimes I want to scribble down half an idea and leave it there. Sometimes I want to work through a problem by writing badly for a few minutes. Sometimes I make a list that will be irrelevant by the end of the afternoon.

I don't need an AI assistant to summarize any of that.

In fact, I like the fact that a plain notepad doesn't encourage me to turn every little thought into a permanent piece of information. It gives me somewhere to think without asking me to do anything with the result.

## Plain text is wonderfully portable

There is another reason I've always liked plain text: it doesn't belong to a particular application.

A plain text note is just text. You can open it almost anywhere, copy it into another application, put it in a Git repository, convert it to Markdown, send it to someone or give it to an AI tool.

That simplicity becomes more valuable as the number of applications we use increases. The fewer assumptions a format makes about where it lives, the easier it is to move around.

I'm not saying everyone should abandon [rich note-taking applications](/rich-text-notes/). There are plenty of situations where structured notes, search, synchronization and collaboration are exactly what you need.

But for quick capture, plain text remains hard to beat.

## Not every note needs to live forever

I think we've also developed a strange obsession with preserving everything. Because storage is cheap and search is powerful, it is tempting to keep every thought, every meeting and every random snippet forever.

But a lot of notes have a short lifespan.

A debugging note might only matter until you've fixed the bug. A shopping list might be useless once you've returned from the store. A rough outline might disappear once you've written the actual article.

That's not a failure of the note. That's the note doing its job.

## Why I built [Notepad.js.org](/)

This is more or less the thinking behind [Notepad.js.org](/). I wanted a notepad that didn't try to turn itself into a complete productivity platform.

You open it and start writing. There is no account to create, no complicated workspace to configure, and no system you have to learn before you can use it.

It works in the browser, can be used offline, and is designed to keep the writing experience quiet and uncluttered.

That's not meant to compete with every feature-rich note-taking application out there. It's meant to do one thing really well: give you a place to write.

## AI will keep getting better. That's okay.

I suspect AI will become a much bigger part of how we deal with information. It will search more of it, organize more of it and probably remember more of it for us.

I'm looking forward to that.

But I don't think it makes the humble notepad obsolete. If anything, having powerful tools available later makes it even more reasonable to keep the first step simple.

Write something down. Don't worry about what it is yet. Decide what it means later.

Sometimes the smartest note-taking workflow is still the one that feels almost too simple to be a workflow at all.
